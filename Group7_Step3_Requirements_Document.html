Requirements Document: Extreme Weather and Transit Ridership Dashboard

Group 7 · Urban Transit Dynamics and Extreme Weather · NYC and Chicago, 2017–2025

This document sets out what the dashboard must do, based on three rounds with stakeholder personas (Alex, a decision-maker at NYC Transit; Shirley, a CTA 'L' commuter; and Rebecca, a skeptical CTA data analyst) and on the analysis in our notebook. Every number below comes from the notebook's cleaned data: NYC subway station-level ridership (MTA), Chicago rail and bus ridership (CTA), and NOAA daily weather.

1. The real need

What our Phase 1 questions assumed

Our Phase 1 questions assumed that the main need was to measure how much ridership falls on extreme-weather days, and that comparing those drops would answer five questions:

  1. Has rail riders' sensitivity to extreme weather changed between 2017–19 and 2022–25?
  2. Is bus ridership affected more than rail ridership in Chicago?
  3. Does extreme heat reduce ridership as much as heavy snow, and does that differ between Chicago and New York?
  4. Do certain stations hold their ridership better on extreme-weather days?
  5. Does the citywide drop look the same in every NYC borough (and Chicago side), or is one area driving it?

Behind these questions were three assumptions: that a drop in ridership shows riders choosing not to travel, that "before vs. after COVID" comparisons would have enough days to answer, and that a borough breakdown would show where to target investment.

What the stakeholder rounds showed

  • Weather is small next to the recovery gap, but bad days still matter. The largest weather effect on annual ridership was about 0.33% (NYC subway, snow, 2017–19); after COVID, no hazard in any system exceeded 0.14%. Meanwhile NYC subway ridership in 2025 was about 74% of its 2019 level. Alex concluded that "weather isn't a revenue problem; ridership recovery is." Rebecca cautioned that an annual share shouldn't be used to dismiss weather, because single bad days are when crowding, service and safety decisions happen.
  • A ridership drop is not the same as riders choosing to stay home. All three personas raised this. Our data can't separate riders staying home from service being cut, delayed or suspended. One case showed how much this matters: the G-line stations that first looked like the largest "heat losers" in NYC were in fact closed during a July–August 2024 service suspension.
  • Many comparisons rest on very few days. Chicago has about 2 extreme-cold weekdays a year (7 per period); NYC had only 2 snow weekdays after COVID. For several before-vs-after comparisons, the honest answer is "we can't tell."
  • Boroughs look alike partly by construction. Heat, cold and snow are measured at one weather station per city, so boroughs can't differ in those hazards. The borough split of riders lost mostly mirrors where riders are (Manhattan about half, Brooklyn about a quarter).

The real need

The dashboard should give an honest, hazard-by-hazard picture of how much lower ridership is on extreme-weather weekdays, how often each hazard happens, and how sure we can be, with the data's limits stated where the numbers appear. It supports two different decisions:

  • Sizing ridership at risk (Alex): rides lost per year and share of annual ridership for each hazard, by borough, next to the recovery gap. It is not a tool for snow-day service levels or capital targeting, which need delivered-service and station-exposure data we don't have.
  • Planning a commute (Shirley): which hazards make the Chicago 'L' noticeably emptier and how often they happen. Cold is the one worth a backup plan; snow, heat and rain change ridership by only a few percent on a typical day. Shirley's resulting rule of thumb: when the forecast daily low is around −1°F or colder (about 2 weekdays a year), have a backup plan ready; otherwise, ride. This is a rule about ridership, not a prediction of delays or service.

Both uses start from the same fact, which the dashboard should state first: weather's effect on annual ridership is small next to the post-COVID recovery gap. Leading with that scale keeps the per-hazard views from being over-read.

3. Main representations

All results compare each extreme-weather weekday with a normal weekday: the median of non-extreme, non-holiday weekdays on the same weekday in the same month. Weekdays from Dec 20 to Jan 2 are left out, and 2020–21 is excluded from comparisons. Hazards are defined as follows: extreme heat is a daily high at or above the hottest 5% of 2017–19 summer days (92°F NYC, 93°F Chicago); extreme cold is a daily low at or below the coldest 5% of 2017–19 winter days (14°F NYC, −1°F Chicago); snow is 2 inches or more; heavy rain is 1 inch or more.

Method changes from the rounds

  • Wording: "weather cut ridership" became "ridership was lower," because ridership can't show whether riders stayed home or service was cut.
  • Ranges: now resample whole weather episodes (weekdays within 3 days of each other count as one event), not single days. Cells with fewer than 8 episodes show individual days instead of a range.
  • Typical values: medians are reported beside means, along with a "without the most extreme day" view, so one closure or blizzard can't carry a result.
  • Calendar days: one rule for both periods. Days next to a federal holiday, plus a CPS non-attendance day and an NYC spring-recess day, are kept, and every result is also shown without them.
  • Day counts: extreme-day counts use the same weekdays as the results (weekdays only, Dec 20–Jan 2 excluded).
  • Comparability: CTA's restated 2025 counts are shaded as not comparable on the trend, and bus vs. rail is also checked on 2022–24 only.
  • Closures: MTA station-days with near-zero ridership are treated as closures, matching CTA's existing rule.

Out of scope with this data

Scheduled vs. delivered service on extreme days; delays, crowding and safety; station flood or heat exposure; storm timing within a day; school-break calendars for every flagged day. These were requested but cannot be answered from ridership and daily weather data. The dashboard should say so rather than leave the question open, and agencies would need to supply service records for a future version.
